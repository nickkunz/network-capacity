%% abstract
\begin{abstract}
\noindent
    From cells to cities, ecosystems to economies, complex networks exhibit peak intensities that define their empirical limit. Estimating these limits traditionally requires domain-specific knowledge. We demonstrate that a wide range of complex networks share a capacity frontier that constrains these maxima. We formalize this regularity by proposing Structural Capacity Theory for Network Substrate Structured Systems. These systems couple a fixed network substrate with an observable dynamical process. The frontier is learned from a vector representation of the network substrate and modulated log-additively by a vector representation of the dynamical process. Frontiers trained on one set of domains reliably bound empirical maxima of entirely different ones without observing their peak intensities or simulating their dynamics. Independent learning paradigms converge on consistent upper bounds. Frontier performance is stable under perturbation, yet degrades under deliberate falsification, with no performance gain under ablation. These findings support the capacity frontier as a generalizable constraint that describes how network structure bounds dynamical processes across otherwise unrelated systems.
\end{abstract}

\phantomsection
\label{sec:intro}
Maxima are fundamental features of complex networks. Understanding them is important because they often mark where critical thresholds are met that cause material changes in network behavior \citep{ref01, ref02, ref16, ref33, ref34}. For example, a regulatory network that exceeds its threshold of localized molecular activity changes regimes \citep{ref25,ref02}. A transportation network that exceeds its carrying capacity changes behavior primarily by congestion, not by design \citep{ref03,ref04}. Although it is known that network maxima can affect network behavior, understanding \textit{how} traditionally requires domain-specific knowledge \citep{ref02,ref03}. Whether or not network maxima have a common regularity across domains is still an open question \citep{ref13,ref14,ref15}. Addressing it requires a shared basis of comparison.

In the previous examples, network structure formed the basis on which a dynamical process occurred \citep{ref02, ref03, ref04, ref05, ref15, ref33}. Size, connectivity, spectral organization, and other topological characteristics constrain network throughput \citep{ref07,ref08,ref28,ref35}, propagation \citep{ref09,ref10,ref30}, synchronization \citep{ref11}, controllability \citep{ref27}, and spatial transport \citep{ref32}. The same network substrate can have different limits depending on the observational definition, activity attribution, temporal aggregation, and measurement regime \citep{ref06,ref26,ref12}. Conversely, structurally distinct substrates and dynamical processes can exhibit comparable limits under shared measurement conventions and operational definitions \citep{ref17,ref18,ref24,ref15}. In both cases, maxima reflect the network substrate and the dynamical process by which observations are attributed, generated, and measured.

\clearpage

We propose Network Substrate Structured Systems (N3S) as a class of systems for which this coupling can be evaluated. An N3S is defined by a fixed network substrate, an observable dynamical process that corresponds to that substrate, and a measurable maximum rate that corresponds to that dynamical process (Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}). Membership is determined from the observations a given system naturally provides so that the scope of the theory is fixed before it is tested. Structural Capacity Theory (SCT) proposes that maxima from \textit{any} N3S are comparable when represented in a shared coordinate system, and within that representation, maxima are constrained by a common capacity frontier.

We estimate the capacity frontier as a covariate-dependent upper-envelope \citep{ref103,ref111}. Typical network activity can be captured by central tendencies, but the area of interest is the ceiling, so the estimators are trained as such \citep{ref19,ref20,ref21,ref22,ref23,ref31,ref29}. The system's network substrate $G$ and dynamical process $S$ are encoded as graph invariants $x$ and process signatures $z$, and standardized to $x'$ and $z'$, respectively. The capacity frontier $F(x', z') = f_C(x') + f_R(z')$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:frontier}) log-additively bounds the log-transformed maximum rate $y^* = \ln(1 + r_{\max})$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:maximum}). The frontier is strictly empirical. It permits a small fraction of observations to cross it and carries no claim of analytical containment.

We evaluate whether this formulation supports a transferable, consistent, stable, falsifiable, and decomposable capacity frontier. We found a frontier that bounded empirical maxima from out-of-sample systems using Leave-One-Group-Out Cross-Validation (LOGO-CV) and repeated $k$-Fold Cross-Validation ($k$-Fold-CV) with equivalent performance. Independent estimators from 3 distinct learning paradigms predicted consistent frontier values. Perturbations to network substrates, graph invariants, dynamical processes, and process signatures preserved frontier performance. By contrast, 3 falsification procedures that deliberately destroyed the representation–target correspondence degraded frontier performance, which retraining could not recover. Finally, ablations that tested simpler and more complex alternative formulations did not meaningfully improve the performance of the frontier.

\label{sec:results}

\subsection*{Shared Coordinate System}
\label{sub-sec:shared}
Encoding all systems as paired network substrate and dynamical process vectors placed unrelated phenomena within a shared coordinate system (Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:si-corpus}). The corpus of 25 systems contained 5 broad domains: Earth \& Physical Sciences, Life Sciences \& Medicine, Technology \& Information, Trade \& Institutions, and Transportation \& Infrastructure. Each of the 5 broad domains contained 5 individual disciplines. Their measurements did not share units before encoding. The network substrate vector $x$ admitted 21 graph invariants (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:si-features}). The dynamical process vector $z$ admitted 7 process signatures (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:si-signatures}). Both were selected under prespecified criteria (\nameref{sec:encoding}) and standardized to $x'$ and $z'$, respectively. This encoding recast every system with the same number of dimensions despite large differences in substance and scale. It made empirical maxima from the entire corpus of 25 systems directly comparable.

Within this shared representation, the capacity frontier bounded empirical maxima across the corpus (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:si-transfer}). Under repeated $k$-Fold-CV, the frontier was fit on each training set and evaluated on the corresponding test set. Each test was repeated 30 times to reduce dependence on any single seeded fold. Frontier performance was reported as medians across estimators after averaging them over test folds and repetitions. Under repeated $5$-Fold-CV and repeated $10$-Fold-CV, frontier efficiency was 0.77 and 0.76, respectively. Frontier efficiency was measured using the Efficiency Index (EI) --- the bounded $[0, 1]$ geometric mean of the non-violation rate and two bounded metrics that decreased with violation magnitude and slack relative to the evaluated target scale (\nameref{sec:performance-metrics},~Eq.~\eqref{eq:ei}). There were infrequent frontier violations, low violation magnitude, and consistent slack. Under repeated 5-Fold-CV, the violation rate was 0.02, mean violation magnitude 0.06, and the mean slack 6.86 log units. Under repeated 10-Fold-CV, the corresponding values were 0.01, 0.02, and 6.87 log units, respectively. 

\subsection*{Transfer Across Domains}
\label{sub-sec:transfer}
A capacity frontier trained without a given domain constrained the empirical maxima of that domain’s disciplines (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:si-transfer}). Under domain-level LOGO-CV, the frontier was trained on 4 of the 5 broad domains and evaluated on the 5 individual disciplines in the withheld domain. Frontier efficiency was 0.75, ranging from 0.70 to 0.91 (Fig.~\ref{fig:transfer}a). The violation rate was 0.04, mean violation magnitude 0.08, and mean slack 6.86 log units. Estimation-observation consensus was 0.84, ranging from 0.77 to 0.92 across the same evaluations (Fig.~\ref{fig:transfer}b). Estimation-observation consensus was measured using the Consensus Index (CI) --- the bounded $[0, 1]$ geometric mean of global rank agreement, high-capacity overlap, and non-linear dependence (\nameref{sec:performance-metrics},~Eq.~\eqref{eq:ci}). Each withheld discipline supplied its own graph invariants, process signatures, and maximum rate. It tested domain transfer by out-of-sample training, not prediction in the absence of observation. This established generalization across the tested domains.

A capacity frontier trained without a given discipline also bounded that discipline’s empirical maxima. Withholding a single discipline tested similar generalization as domain-level LOGO-CV, but at a higher resolution. Under discipline-level LOGO-CV the frontier was trained on 24 of the 25 disciplines and evaluated on the one that was withheld, effectively Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOO-CV). In this resampling regime frontier efficiency was 0.73, ranging from 0.52 to 0.90 across disciplines. The violation rate was 0.00, mean violation magnitude 0.00, and mean slack 6.12 log units. Estimation-observation consensus was 0.82 based on out-of-sample predictions pooled within each of the 5 broad domains, ranging from 0.77 to 0.92 across domains. Violations were less common and smaller than under domain-level LOGO-CV. The experiment could not determine whether these differences reflected denser coverage of the coordinate system by in-sample disciplines or differences in test-fold size and metric aggregation. Nevertheless, the frontier generalized to out-of-sample disciplines similarly to out-of-sample domains.

The capacity frontier achieved equivalent frontier performance across resampling regimes. Using the repeated $k$-Fold-CV results (\nameref{sub-sec:shared}), paired differences in frontier efficiency between domain-level LOGO-CV and repeated $k$-Fold-CV were tested across disciplines within the 5 broad domains (Fig.~\ref{fig:transfer}c). Paired differences were computed by subtracting domain-level LOGO-CV results from those contained in the comparative resampling regime, where negative values indicated lower performance. Median paired differences in frontier efficiency were 0.00 for all resampling regimes. Median paired differences in estimation-observation consensus were at most 0.01 in magnitude under the same evaluation. Paired Wilcoxon Two One-Sided Tests (TOST) across 45 estimator-by-domain comparisons supported equivalence within their empirically derived margins ($\delta_{\mathrm{EI}} = 0.02$ and $\delta_{\mathrm{CI}} = 0.11$, all Holm-adjusted $p < 0.001$; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:si-transfer-equivalence}). Withholding broad domains and individual disciplines preserved frontier performance when compared to repeated random resampling. Thus, domain generalization did not depend on any single tested resampling regime.

\subsection*{Consensus Across Paradigms}
\label{sub-sec:consensus}
The capacity frontier was consistent across learning paradigms (Fig.~\ref{fig:consensus}). There were 9 independent estimators across 3 distinct learning paradigms, all of which broadly agreed on their predicted frontier values. The learning paradigms were linear parametric, non-linear ensemble, and neural networks. All models were trained on the same inputs $x'$ and $z'$ while the estimators differed in functional form, regularization, and optimization. Each test was repeated 30 times to reduce sensitivity to parameter initialization and optimization stochasticity. Predicted frontier values were averaged across repetitions. Pairwise model consensus across all 36 pairs of seed-averaged predictions was 0.90 (Fig.~\ref{fig:consensus}a). A geometric mean this close to 1 simultaneously required high rank agreement, top-weighted overlap, and statistical dependence, such that no single component could carry the outcome. Rescaled Spearman rank correlation ($\rho_s^{+}$), Rank-Biased Overlap (RBO), and distance correlation ($\mathrm{dCor}$) were 0.95, 0.86, and 0.91, respectively. The agreement was evaluated on in-sample analyses using full-corpus fits. Consensus across the tested learning paradigms indicated that the frontier was unlikely an artifact of inductive bias (Fig.~\ref{fig:consensus}b).

%% stress tests
\subsection*{Stable Under Perturbation}
\label{sub-sec:perturb}
The capacity frontier was stable under perturbation (Fig.~\ref{fig:tests}a, Fig.~\ref{fig:tests}b; Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:si-perturb} and \ref{tab:si-protocol}). There were 12 perturbation procedures across 4 categories that introduced controlled alterations to the vectors $x'$ and $z'$ while preserving the target $y^*$ in distinct ways: network substrates, graph invariants, dynamical processes, and process signatures. Under domain-level LOGO-CV, the frontier was trained on the original data, the model frozen, then evaluated on the perturbed data in the withheld domain. Each test was repeated 30 times and averaged across repetitions to reduce sensitivity to perturbation realization and estimator initialization. Paired differences were computed by subtracting the original results from the perturbed ones across 45 estimator-by-domain comparisons, where negative values indicated lower performance. At the highest tested intensity, median paired differences in frontier efficiency ranged from -0.02 to +0.01. Median paired differences in estimation-observation consensus were 0.00 for all network and invariant perturbations. Median paired differences in consensus were -0.11 for process bootstrapping, -0.02 for process scaling, and -0.06 for process smoothing. Signature jitter, noise, and subset produced median consensus differences of -0.01, -0.01, and -0.04, respectively. Paired Wilcoxon TOST across 45 estimator-by-domain comparisons supported equivalence for all 12 perturbations within their empirically derived margins ($\delta_{\mathrm{EI}} = 0.08$ and $\delta_{\mathrm{CI}} = 0.14$, all Holm-adjusted $p \leq 0.028$; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:si-perturb}). Response curves across normalized perturbation intensities were descriptive, where only the highest perturbation intensity was formally tested. Nevertheless, the frontier could withstand structural alterations, process distortions, and feature noise while remaining reliable.

\subsection*{Degradation Under Falsification}
\label{sub-sec:falsify}
The capacity frontier predictably degraded under falsification (Fig.~\ref{fig:tests}c; Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:si-falsify} and \ref{tab:si-protocol}). There were 3 falsification procedures that deliberately destroyed the correspondence between the vector representations $x'$ and $z'$ and the target $y^*$ in distinct ways: target permutation, random generation, and vector generation. Under domain-level LOGO-CV, the frontier was evaluated by freezing the models after training on the original data, and separately after retraining on the falsified data. Each test was repeated 30 times and averaged across repetitions to reduce sensitivity to estimator initialization and training stochasticity. Paired differences were computed by subtracting the original results from the falsified ones across 45 estimator-by-domain comparisons, where negative values indicated lower performance. Under the frozen model, median paired differences in frontier efficiency were -0.02 for target permutation, -0.04 for random generation, and -0.03 for vector generation. After retraining, median paired differences in frontier efficiency were -0.09, -0.02, and -0.03, respectively. Median paired differences in estimation-observation consensus were -0.29, -0.28, and -0.21 when frozen, and -0.24, -0.18, and -0.31 after retraining, respectively. Paired One-Sided Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Tests supported lower efficiency and lower consensus across all 12 comparisons (all Holm-adjusted $p \le 0.013$ for EI and $p < 0.001$ for CI; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:si-falsify}). The falsified corpora served as negative controls that indicated the frontier depended on a true representation–target correspondence.

\subsection*{Parsimony Under Ablation}
\label{sub-sec:ablate}
No alternative capacity frontier formulation meaningfully outperformed the log-additive decomposition (Fig.~\ref{fig:tests}d; Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:si-ablation} and \ref{tab:si-protocol}). The 4 alternative specifications contained simple ablations: invariants only and signatures only, and complex ablations: joint features and interaction terms. Invariants only trained the frontier as $F(x')$ alone, while signatures only used $F(z')$ alone. Joint features concatenated both vectors into a single-stage fit $F([x', z'])$, while interaction terms augmented the log-additive decomposition as $F(x', z', h')$ with pairwise products $x_j z_k$. Under domain-level LOGO-CV, ablations were fit on each training set and evaluated on the corresponding test set. Each test was repeated 30 times and averaged across repetitions to reduce sensitivity to estimator initialization and training stochasticity. Paired differences were computed by subtracting the original results from the ablated ones across 45 estimator-by-domain comparisons, where negative values indicated lower performance. Median paired differences in frontier efficiency were -0.02 for joint features, and -0.01 for invariants only, signatures only, and interaction terms. Median paired differences in estimation-observation consensus were -0.20 for invariants only, -0.08 for signatures only, -0.27 for joint features, and -0.10 for interaction terms. Paired One-Sided Wilcoxon Signed-Rank tests supported non-inferiority of the log-additive decomposition to all 4 alternatives in both metrics within their empirically derived margins ($\delta_{\mathrm{EI}} = 0.08$ and $\delta_{\mathrm{CI}} = 0.14$, all Holm-adjusted $p < 0.001$; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:si-ablation}). These tests limited alternatives and supported the log-additive decomposition as the parsimonious formulation of the frontier.

\section*{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The capacity frontier constrains maximum rates across disciplinary boundaries. A frontier with either a broad domain or individual discipline withheld from training bounded maximum rates with performance comparable to repeated random resampling. Information from one system was therefore useful for estimating the maxima in another despite differences in subject, size, scale, units, and dynamics. These comparisons provide a basis for asking which constraints are shared across systems and which depend on domain specificity. Capacity estimation can therefore use empirical evidence beyond a system’s own substantive boundary.

Furthermore, the capacity frontier does not require a model of the dynamical processes. Graph invariants and process signatures support capacity estimation without training domain specific models or simulating their dynamics. Domain specific models are still important for causal explanation and intervention, but SCT provides a standard benchmark as a complement. Interpreting this benchmark requires additional inquiry, as the reported slack was nearly 3 orders of magnitude higher than the maxima. This gap may reflect a loose envelope, unequal observation windows, differences in measurements, or a combination of them. Although the meaning of the slack is still unclear, the frontier still reliably bounds maximum rates without a traditional model of the underlying dynamics.

Selective stability demonstrates why the capacity frontier is informative. Perturbations at the highest tested intensity generally preserved the frontier's original performance. Falsifications that deliberately destroyed the representation–target correspondence degraded frontier performance, which was unrecoverable even after retraining. Consensus among learning paradigms was relatively high and comparatively similar between the original and perturbed conditions. Only after falsification and ablation did the model consensus between learning paradigms degrade. This supports the frontier as a non-spurious object, distinguishable from a fragile upper-envelope.

The capacity frontier is jointly determined and decomposable. Removing either component or combining them without log-additivity did not improve frontier performance or consensus among estimators. The results support the log-additive decomposition, but leave its exhaustiveness unresolved. All findings were conditioned on tested 25 systems, 21 graph invariants, 7 process signatures, 9 learned estimators, the log-additive decomposition, and the daily temporal aggregation. Broader corpora, different vector representations, more diverse learning paradigms, and alternative temporal scales all provide direct tests of SCT.

\section*{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection*{N3S Definition}
Within SCT, an N3S contains a fixed network substrate $G$, an observable dynamical process $S$, and a measurable maximum rate $r_{\max}$. The network substrate contains finite elements with relational or attribution structure. The dynamical process is a time-indexed sequence of observed events or reconstructed events from published schedules. Each event is attributable or relates to one or more elements of the network substrate. A given measurement convention converts the sequence into an empirical rate series. Membership requires records for all 3 components. Operational recastings are fixed across all reported evaluations. Systems without an identifiable substrate or with only aggregate observations lacking substrate attribution are excluded from analyses (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:si-corpus}).

\subsection*{Data and Corpus Composition}
The corpus contained 25 systems across 5 broad domains: Earth \& Physical Sciences, Life Sciences \& Medicine, Technology \& Information, Trade \& Institutions, and Transportation \& Infrastructure. Each of the 5 broad domains contained 5 individual disciplines (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:si-corpus}; \hyperref[sec:si-note-processing]{Supplementary Note 2}). Network substrates ranged from 5 to 10,356,390 nodes and from 10 to 7,220,486,175 edges. Maximum rates ranged from 3 to 1,323,743 observations per day. Observation windows ranged from 30 to 8,014 days. Measurements shared no units prior to encoding. Each system contributed exactly one record containing its graph invariants $x$, process signatures $z$, and maximum rate $r_{\max}$.

\subsection*{Network Substrate Construction}
Network substrates were represented as static, unweighted, and undirected graphs. They were strictly held fixed across their observation windows. Nodes represented units. Edges represented native connections between nodes or constructed associations. Directed and weighted edges were converted to undirected and unweighted edges. Multi-edges and self-loops were removed. Isolated nodes were retained. Each network substrate was formally defined as a simple graph $G$:
\begin{equation}
    G \;=\; (V, E),
    \qquad
    E \subseteq \binom{V}{2}
\end{equation}
Let $\mathcal{O}$ denote the observations from which the dynamical process $S$ was constructed. An attribution map assigned each observation $o \in \mathcal{O}$ a nonempty set $a(o) \subseteq V \cup E$, where $V$ and $E$ denote the node and edge sets, respectively. Interaction logs formed unions of observed pairs or complete bipartite graphs over documented entity types. There were 4 network substrates (college, email, mooc, overflow) defined as complete bipartite graphs $K_{m,n}$ by sizes $m$ and $n$ (\hyperref[sec:si-note-bipartite]{Supplementary Note 1}). Each $G$ served as the fixed network substrate to which observations of the system's dynamical process were attributed.

\subsection*{Dynamical Process Construction}
Dynamical processes were constructed from empirical observations.
For continuous records, measurements were discretized using system-specific thresholds (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:si-corpus}). Event counts were aggregated into uniform daily intervals using UTC timestamps or consecutive 24-hour periods. Note the exception of 1 system (celegans) where developmental transitions were reconstructed from published timings using a 30-day dauer duration. Each dynamical process was formally defined as a chronologically ordered discrete daily rate series $S$:
\begin{equation}
    S \;=\; \left\{ r_{t} \right\}_{t \in T},
    \qquad
    r_{t} \;=\; \frac{n_{t}}{\Delta t},
    \qquad
    \Delta t \;=\; 1\ \mathrm{day}\ (24\ \mathrm{hours})
    \label{eq:series}
\end{equation}

where $n_{t}$ is the event count in interval $t$ and $T$ is the chronologically ordered set of intervals. The number of included intervals was $L = \lvert T \rvert$, with series mean $\bar{r} = L^{-1}\sum_{t \in T} r_t$. Source conventions determined interval inclusion. For 19 systems, $T$ contained only days with at least one recorded event. There were 5 systems that supplied regular snapshots aggregated into consecutive daily intervals, including zero-event days (metrla, pemsbay, montevideo, windmill, and wiki) and 1 system (chickenpox) that computed a daily-average rate at each weekly report date. Each $S$ served as the realization of the system's dynamical process across its observation window.

\subsection*{Maximum Rate Target}
The target value for each system was the log-transformed maximum rate realized across its observation window. The log-transformation compressed the range of empirical maxima. For each rate series $S$ on interval set $T$, the maximum rate $r_{\max}$ and log-transformed maximum rate target $y^*$ are formally defined as:
\begin{equation}
    y^{*} \;=\; \ln\!\left(1 + r_{\max}\right),
    \qquad
    r_{\max} \;=\; \max_{t \in T} r_t
\label{eq:maximum}
\end{equation}
Maxima can grow with observation window length \citep{ref58,ref59,ref60}. Observation windows varied across sources and were held at their recorded durations. All frontiers remained conditional on their recorded observational windows (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:si-corpus}). Each $y^*$ served as the target bounded by the capacity frontier across its observation window.

\subsection*{Encoding and Standardization}
\label{sec:encoding}
Each network substrate was encoded using 21 graph invariants across 5 categories: simple, cohesion, extremal, statistical, and spectral. The mapping $G \to x$ is formally defined as:
\begin{equation}
    x \;=\; \phi(G) \;=\; (x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_{21}) \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{21}
\end{equation}
For the 4 complete bipartite substrates (college, email, mooc, overflow), invariants were evaluated using exact closed-form expressions for $K_{m,n}$ to avoid instantiating billions of edges.

Each dynamical process was encoded using 7 process signatures across 5 categories: dependence, dispersion, volatility, trend, and asymmetry. The mapping $S \to z$ is formally defined as:
\begin{equation}
    z \;=\; \psi(S) \;=\; (z_1, z_2, \ldots, z_7) \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{7}
\end{equation}
6 process signatures were scale-free. The Fano factor retained count scale, equaling the series mean $\bar{r}$ multiplied by the squared coefficient of variation, reflecting process dispersion.

$x$ and $z$ were centered by in-sample means and scaled by in-sample population standard deviations, with 0 standard deviations replaced by 1. The standardized vectors are formally defined as:
\begin{equation}
x' \;=\; \frac{x - \mu_x}{\sigma_x},
\qquad
z' \;=\; \frac{z - \mu_z}{\sigma_z}
\end{equation}
Means and scales were fit strictly in-sample (Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:si-features}--\ref{tab:si-signatures}). 

All features met the admission criteria: classical, deterministic, global to the network or series, free of arbitrary thresholds, without external parameters, and finite.

\subsection*{Frontier Learning}
The capacity frontier was learned through a two-stage log-additive formulation. The structural stage (stage 1) was $f_C(x')$, and the process stage (stage 2) was $f_R(z')$. The same estimator type was trained separately in each stage. Hyperparameters were held constant. The frontier is formally defined as:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{y}^* = F(x', z') = f_C(x'; \tau_C) + f_R(z'; \tau_R)
\label{eq:frontier}
\end{equation}
\clearpage

Stage 1 trained the structural component $f_C(x'; \tau_C)$ to target $y^{*}$ via asymmetric regression at $\tau_C = 0.99$. It placed the learned function near the upper-envelope given the graph invariants \citep{ref29}. Stage 2 trained the process component $f_R(z'; \tau_R)$ to the signed slack $y^{*} - f_C(x'; \tau_C)$ at $\tau_R = 0.50$. Subtracting the process stage's training mean $\mu_R$ fixed its additive offset \citep{ref67}, resulting in the centered contribution $f_R(z'; \tau_R)$ while leaving $f_C(x'; \tau_C)$ unchanged. Summing both stages yielded the frontier estimate $\hat{y}^*$. $\tau$ denotes the active stage's asymmetry level $\tau_C$ (stage 1) and $\tau_R$ (stage 2).

The linear parametric paradigm included linear quantile regression \citep{ref22} minimizing mean pinball loss with $\ell_1$-penalized slopes ($\alpha = 0.1$), unpenalized intercepts in both stages, and the \texttt{HiGHS 1.8.0} solver. Linear Asymmetric Weighted Least Squares (LAWS) minimized $(2n)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i e_i^2 + \alpha\lVert\beta\rVert_1$ ($\alpha = 0.1$), with $w_i = \tau$ for $e_i \ge 0$ and $1-\tau$ otherwise \citep{ref36}. Here $e_i$ is the active stage's observed training response minus its prediction, $n$ the training sample size, and $\beta$ the slope vector. Cyclic coordinate descent used zero initial slopes, tolerance $10^{-4}$, and at most 1,000 passes \citep{ref76}. LAWS used an unpenalized stage 1 intercept initialized at the target mean and no stage 2 intercept. The concave-envelope regressor fitted a stage-specific linear quantile score ($\alpha = 0.1$) and interpolated the concave upper-envelope of target values with constant extrapolation beyond endpoints.

The non-linear ensemble paradigm included a quantile random forest with 100 fully grown bootstrap trees, squared-error splits, a minimum leaf size of 1, and all predictors eligible at each split \citep{ref37}. Forest predictions were quantiles of the fitted conditional target distribution. Gradient-boosted quantile regression minimized pinball loss using 100 trees with maximum depth 3, learning rate 0.1, and the full training sample \citep{ref38}. XGBoost minimized pinball loss with histogram tree construction, 100 trees, maximum depth 6, learning rate 0.3, an $\ell_2$ penalty $\lambda = 1$, and full row and feature sampling \citep{ref39}. Each estimator used the quantile assigned to the active stage.

The neural network paradigm included an MLP with pinball loss, an MLP with expectile loss, and an input-convex network with pinball loss \citep{ref40}. Non-input weights in the convex network were made non-negative through absolute values. All networks used hidden layers of 8 and 4 units, ReLU activations \citep{ref80}, dropout of 0.1 \citep{ref77}, and output biases initialized at the active stage's training quantile. Full-batch AdamW optimization used learning rate 0.1, weight decay 0.01, gradient-norm clipping at 5.0, and 1,000 epochs \citep{ref41}. Each stage's predictions were clipped to its own training target range before process centering and summation.

\subsection*{Performance Metrics}
\phantomsection
\label{sec:performance-metrics}
Frontier efficiency was measured using the Efficiency Index (EI). EI is the geometric mean of the non-violation rate $(1 - \mathrm{VR})$, the bounded mean-violation score $\smash{{(1 + \mathrm{MV}/\overline{\lvert y^{*} \rvert})}^{-1}}$, and the bounded mean-slack score $\smash{{(1 + \mathrm{MS}/\overline{\lvert y^{*} \rvert})}^{-1}}$, formally defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{EI} \;=\;
    \Big[
        (1 - \mathrm{VR}) \cdot
        \Big{(1 + \mathrm{MV}/\overline{\lvert y^{*} \rvert}\Big)}^{-1} \cdot
        \Big{(1 + \mathrm{MS}/\overline{\lvert y^{*} \rvert}\Big)}^{-1}
    \Big]^{1/3} \in [0, 1]
    \label{eq:ei}
\end{equation}
For $n$ evaluated systems with observed targets $y_i^{*}$ and predicted values $\hat{y}_i^{*}$, let $\mathcal{V} = \{i : y_i^{*} > \hat{y}_i^{*}\}$ denote the violators. The Violation Rate (VR) is the fraction of evaluated systems exceeding their predictions, $\mathrm{VR} = \lvert \mathcal{V} \rvert/n$. The Mean Violation (MV) is the mean exceedance among violators, $\mathrm{MV} = \big(\sum_{i \in \mathcal{V}} (y_i^{*} - \hat{y}_i^{*})\big)/\lvert \mathcal{V} \rvert$ (0 when $\lvert \mathcal{V} \rvert = 0$). The Mean Slack (MS) is the mean non-negative gap between $\hat{y}_i^{*}$ and $y_i^{*}$ across all evaluated systems, $\mathrm{MS} = n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \max(\hat{y}_i^{*} - y_i^{*}, 0)$. MV and MS are normalized by the sample mean absolute target $\overline{\lvert y^{*} \rvert} = n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \lvert y_i^{*} \rvert$. For numerical stability, $10^{-10}$ was added to the normalization denominator and used as a floor for $1 - \mathrm{VR}$. MV and MS are expressed in natural-log units. Note that EI = 1 under perfect frontier efficiency, which requires no violations and no slack (VR = MV = MS = 0).
\clearpage

Frontier consensus was measured using the Consensus Index (CI). CI is the geometric mean of rescaled Spearman rank correlation $\rho_s^{+} = (1 + \rho_s)/2$ \citep{ref42}, top-weighted Rank-Biased Overlap (RBO) \citep{ref43}, and distance correlation ($\mathrm{dCor}$) \citep{ref44}, formally defined as:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CI} \;=\;
\Big[
\rho_s^{+} \cdot \mathrm{RBO} \cdot \mathrm{dCor}
\Big]^{1/3} \in [0, 1]
\label{eq:ci}
\end{equation}
$\rho_s$ used average ranks for ties and was set to 0 when either vector was constant, yielding $\rho_s^{+} = 0.5$. RBO evaluated descending rankings with persistence $p_{\mathrm{RBO}} = 0.9$. Ties were resolved by ascending input index. RBO used tail extrapolation from agreement at the last evaluated depth. $\mathrm{dCor}$ used the biased V-statistic sample estimator with Euclidean-distance exponent 1 and 0 for a constant vector. All CI components were clipped $[0, 1]$ against floating point imprecision. CI measured estimation-observation consensus between predicted values $\hat{y}^{*}$ and observed targets $y^*$ as well as pairwise model consensus between predicted values $\hat{y}^{*}_i$ and $\hat{y}^{*}_j$ from distinct estimators $i \neq j$. Note that CI = 1 under perfect frontier consensus, which requires that all three components be in perfect agreement ($\rho_s^{+} = \mathrm{RBO} = \mathrm{dCor} = 1$).

EI and CI serve as paired metrics designed to measure complementary aspects of frontier performance. EI measures boundary containment and envelope tightness by penalizing violations and excessive slack. CI measures system-specific fidelity by measuring monotonic ordering, top-capacity overlap, and statistical dependence. Critically, neither metric is sufficient in isolation. An arbitrarily high and uninformative constant frontier above all observations can achieve a noteworthy EI despite its slack, yet degrades consensus. Conversely, an uncalibrated ranking can achieve high CI while failing to bound the data. A valid capacity frontier must simultaneously demonstrate containment and fidelity.

\subsection*{Resampling and Leakage Control}
Generalization was evaluated using 4 resampling regimes: repeated $5$-Fold-CV, repeated $10$-Fold-CV, domain-level LOGO-CV, and discipline-level LOGO-CV. Repeated $k$-Fold-CV used random reshuffling over 30 repetitions \citep{ref73}. Domain-level LOGO-CV withheld 1 of the 5 broad domains for each domain. Discipline-level LOGO-CV withheld 1 of the 25 disciplines for each discipline \citep{ref68,ref69,ref70,ref74,ref107}. LOGO-CV folds were deterministic. Repetitions were drawn across training seeds $42 + b$ for $b = 0, \ldots, 29$.

Within each fold, standardizers for $x$ and $z$, both estimators $f_C(.)$ and $f_R(.)$, and the process centering mean $\mu_R$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:frontier}) were fit \textit{exclusively} on the training set. Estimators were freshly cloned on every fold. At inference time, models $f_C(x')$ and $f_R(z')$ had no access to the target $y^*$ or raw rate series $S$. Recall that $y^*$ was log-transformed, which did not require parameter fitting. Therefore, information from out-of-sample systems did not enter standardization and model training on the original corpus during cross-validation.

Falsified negative-control corpora were generated from the full corpus, and feature-wise sample standard deviations for additive-noise perturbations were computed across the full corpus before cross-validation. These corpus-wide operations specified the negative controls and scales related to additive-noise. Across all cross-validated stress tests, standardizers and estimators were fit \textit{exclusively} on the corresponding training set.

\subsection*{Perturbation, Falsification, Ablation}
Network substrate perturbations at intensity $\epsilon$ modified $G = (V, E)$ across prespecified parameter grids and protocols (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:si-protocol}). Rewiring used $\widetilde{G} = \mathcal{R}_q(G)$, where $\mathcal{R}_q$ performed $q = \max(1, \lfloor \epsilon\lvert E \rvert \rfloor)$ degree-preserving trials on a simple graph \citep{ref56}. Densification used $\widetilde{G} = (V, E \cup A)$, where $A$ was sampled uniformly without replacement from $\binom{V}{2} \setminus E$, with $\lvert A \rvert = \min\{\lfloor \epsilon\lvert E \rvert \rfloor, \binom{\lvert V \rvert}{2} - \lvert E \rvert\}$ \citep{ref106}. Node removal used the induced subgraph $\widetilde{G} = G[V \setminus U]$, where $U$ was sampled uniformly without replacement from $V$ and $\lvert U \rvert = \lfloor \epsilon\lvert V \rvert \rfloor$ \citep{ref104,ref105}. Graph invariants were recomputed as $\widetilde{x} = \phi(\widetilde{G})$. For the 4 complete bipartite substrates, node removal used exact closed-form invariants. Rewiring and densification used deterministic approximations (\hyperref[sec:si-note-bipartite]{Supplementary Note 1}).

Dynamical process perturbations modified the rate series through power-law scaling, trailing-mean smoothing, and bootstrapping. For these expressions, $r_{\ell}$ denotes the rate at chronological position $\ell = 1, \ldots, L$, where $L = \lvert T \rvert$. Scaling used $\widetilde{r}_{\ell} = (r_{\ell} + 1)^{\gamma} - 1$. Smoothing used $\widetilde{r}_{\ell} = (\sum_{h=\max(1,\ell-w+1)}^{\ell} r_h)/\min(w,\ell)$, shortening the $w$-interval window at the beginning of the series. Both methods were deterministic. Bootstrapping drew independent indices $I_h \sim \operatorname{Unif}\{1, \ldots, L\}$ for $h = 1, \ldots, k$ \citep{ref75}, with $k = \max(1, \operatorname{round}(sL))$ and halves rounded to even. The first $k$ intervals were $\widetilde{r}_{\ell} = r_{I_{\ell}}$ for $\ell \le k$. The remaining intervals were $\widetilde{r}_{\ell} = r_{I_{J_{\ell}}}$ for $\ell > k$, with independent $J_{\ell} \sim \operatorname{Unif}\{1, \ldots, k\}$. Process signatures were recomputed as $\widetilde{z} = \psi(\widetilde{S})$, where $\widetilde{S}$ was the modified series. All perturbations retained the original targets.

Graph invariants and process signatures were also perturbed through additive noise, multiplicative jitter, and coordinate subsetting. These methods were applied separately to $x$ and $z$ prior to standardization. For either vector $v \in \{x, z\}$, additive noise used $\widetilde{v}_j = v_j + \varepsilon_j$, with $\varepsilon_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, (s\sigma_j)^2)$ and $\sigma_j$ the corpus-wide sample standard deviation of coordinate $j$. Jitter used $\widetilde{v}_j = \max\{v_j(1 + \eta_j), 0\}$ for originally non-negative $v_j$ and $\widetilde{v}_j = v_j(1 + \eta_j)$ otherwise, with $\eta_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, s^2)$. Masking used $\widetilde{v}_j = v_j\mathbf{1}[j \in M]$, where $M$ was a uniformly sampled subset of retained coordinates with $\lvert M \rvert = \lfloor \rho p \rfloor$. Here $\rho$ was the retention fraction, $p = 21$ for $x$ or $p = 7$ for $z$. Each system used the same seed within a realization, sharing coordinate-wise noise and jitter draws and the masked subset across systems.

The 3 falsification procedures deliberately destroyed the mapping $(x', z') \to y^*$. Let $i = 1, \ldots, 25$ index systems and $D_i$ denote their broad domains. Target permutation used $\widetilde{y}_i^{*} = y_{\pi(i)}^{*}$, where $\pi$ was a permutation satisfying $D_{\pi(i)} \ne D_i$, preserving each system's original $x$ and $z$ and the target's marginal distribution. For either raw vector $v \in \{x, z\}$, random generation independently sampled $\widetilde{v}_{ij} \sim \mathcal{U}(a_j, b_j)$, where $a_j = \min_h v_{hj}$ and $b_j = \max_h v_{hj}$ across the corpus. Vector generation resampled empirical coordinates with replacement \citep{ref75} and added Gaussian noise as $\widetilde{v}_{ij} = v_{B_{ij},j} + \xi_{ij}$, with independent $B_{ij} \sim \operatorname{Unif}\{1, \ldots, 25\}$ and $\xi_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \nu_j^2)$. Here $\nu_j$ was the coordinate's corpus-wide population standard deviation, floored at $10^{-10}$. Both feature-generation procedures retained each system's original target.

The 4 ablations compared alternative specifications with the log-additive decomposition using the original training folds and standard estimator protocols. Invariants only used $F_{\mathrm{Inv}}(x') = f_{\mathrm{Inv}}(x'; \tau_C)$. Signatures only used $F_{\mathrm{Sig}}(z') = f_{\mathrm{Sig}}(z'; \tau_C)$. Joint features used $F_{\mathrm{Joint}}(x', z') = f_{\mathrm{Joint}}([x', z']; \tau_C)$, where brackets denote vector concatenation. These ablations used single-stage fits at $\tau_C = 0.99$. Interaction terms formed a 147-coordinate vector $h = {(x_j z_k)}_{j=1,\ldots,21\,;\,k=1,\ldots,7}$ from raw coordinates and standardized it within each training fold to $h'$. The alternative frontier was $F_{\mathrm{Int}}(x', z', h') = f_{C,\mathrm{Int}}(x'; \tau_C) + f_{R,\mathrm{Int}}([z', h']; \tau_R)$, with both stages refit and $f_{R,\mathrm{Int}}$ centered (Eq.~\eqref{eq:frontier}).

\subsection*{Evaluation and Statistical Inference}
\phantomsection
\label{sec:si-stat-conventions}
Inference used 45 estimator-by-domain comparisons summarized over 30 repetitions. Within each domain, EI was computed using seed-averaged predictions for resampling equivalence and falsification. Perturbation and ablation used repetition means in both the original and tested conditions. CI used seed-averaged predictions except for stochastic perturbations, where metrics were averaged after computing each paired training seed and realization.

Paired differences $\Delta\mathrm{EI}$ and $\Delta\mathrm{CI}$ were computed as test comparison minus baseline and summarized by their median. Baselines were domain-level LOGO-CV for resampling equivalence, the original condition for perturbation and falsification, and the log-additive decomposition for ablation. All comparisons used One-Sided Wilcoxon Signed-Rank tests \citep{ref45}, assuming symmetric paired-difference distributions with location parameter $\theta$. Inference was conditional on the given panel whose shared estimators and cross-validation folds induced dependence that may affect $p$-value calibration.

For a margin $\delta > 0$, resampling equivalence and perturbation stability used TOST~\citep{ref47} of $H_{01}: \theta \le -\delta$ and $H_{02}: \theta \ge \delta$. Equivalence required rejection of both nulls with the TOST $p$-value taken as the larger of the 2 one-sided values. Falsification tested degradation ($H_0: \theta \ge 0$ versus $H_1: \theta < 0$). Ablation tested non-inferiority of the log-additive decomposition to each alternative \citep{ref78} ($H_0: \theta \ge \delta$ versus $H_1: \theta < \delta$).

All margins $\delta$ were empirically derived based on interquartile ranges (IQR) rounded down 2 decimal places (Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:si-transfer-equivalence}--\ref{tab:si-perturb} and \ref{tab:si-ablation}). Holm-Bonferroni adjustments (Holm-adjusted $p$, denoted $p_{\mathrm H}$) \citep{ref46} used a significance threshold of $\alpha = 0.05$ separately for EI and CI within 5 families: resampling, perturbation, falsification, simple ablations, and complex ablations.

Rank-biserial effect sizes were computed as $r = (W_{+} - W_{-})/(W_{+} + W_{-})$ from nonzero, unshifted paired differences \citep{ref71}. Here $W_{+}$ and $W_{-}$ are the sums of ranks of absolute differences with positive and negative signs, respectively, with average ranks for ties.

Full-corpus consensus tests were descriptive (Fig.~\ref{fig:consensus}). Each estimator's predictions were averaged across training seeds. For stochastic perturbations, realizations were paired before computing estimation-observation and pairwise model CI. Pairwise model consensus was the median CI across the 36 distinct estimator pairs. Original data fits were frozen for evaluation on the highest intensity perturbed corpora and the falsified corpora. Ablations were refit on the full original corpus.

\subsection*{Supplementary Note 1: Complete Bipartite Perturbations}
\phantomsection
\label{sec:si-note-bipartite}
The 4 complete bipartite substrates (college, email, mooc, overflow) were represented by their fold sizes $m$ and $n$, with $N = m + n$ nodes and $M = mn$ edges, because their explicit graphs reach up to 7,200,000,000 edges. Node removal at intensity $\epsilon$ deleted $\lfloor \epsilon N \rfloor$ nodes drawn uniformly without replacement, counted the removed nodes in each fold, and evaluated the closed forms (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:si-features}) for the remaining complete bipartite graph. This computation is exact and used 30 realizations. Rewiring and densification used a deterministic degree-class approximation with 1 realization.

For degree classes $c$ with degree $d_c$ and $N_c$ nodes, and an edge count $M'$, let
\begin{equation*}
N = \sum_c N_c, \quad S_1 = \sum_c N_c d_c, \quad S_2 = \sum_c N_c d_c^{2}, \quad \bar{d} = \frac{S_1}{N}, \quad \kappa = \frac{S_2}{\max(S_1, 1)} - 1, \quad \overline{d^{-1}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_c \frac{N_c}{\max(d_c, 1)}.
\end{equation*}
The approximation $\Phi$ assigned the following values and set articulation points, bridges, clustering, and assortativity to 0.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
\item Size: $|V| = N$, $|E| = M'$, and $d_{\max} = \max_c d_c$.
\item Cohesion: diameter $D = \max\{2, \ln \max(N, 2) / \ln \kappa\}$ when $\kappa > 1$ and $D = N$ otherwise, radius $D/2$, degeneracy $k^{*} = \min(d_{\max}, \sqrt{2M'})$, and $k$-core size $\sum_c N_c \mathbf{1}[d_c \ge k^{*}]$.
\item Degree statistics: variance $N^{-1} \sum_c N_c (d_c - \bar{d})^2$, bias-corrected skewness and excess kurtosis of the class-weighted degrees, degree entropy $-\sum_k p_k \ln p_k$ over degrees rounded to integers $k$ with vertex fractions $p_k$, and joint degree entropy $-2 \sum_k q_k \ln q_k$ with stub fractions $q_k \propto k p_k$.
\item Spectral moments: $\mathrm{tr}(L^2)/N = 1 + 2M'N / \max(S_1, 1)^2$, $\mathrm{tr}(L^3)/N = 1 + (S_2 - S_1)^3 / \max(N S_1^3, 1)$, $\mathrm{tr}(P^3)/N = 6 (S_2 - S_1)^2\, \overline{d^{-1}} / [\max(6 S_1, 1) \max(S_1, 1)]$, $\mathrm{tr}(P^4)/N = N^{-1} \sum_c N_c [d_c S_2 / \max(S_1^2, 1) + \max(\kappa, 0) / \max(d_c, 1)]$, and $\mathrm{tr}(A^4)/N = [S_2 + S_2^2 / \max(2 S_1, 1)] / N$.
\end{itemize}
Rewiring at intensity $\epsilon$ evaluated $\Phi$ on the classes of $K_{m,n}$, with degrees $(n, m)$, class sizes $(m, n)$, and $M' = M$, and interpolated each invariant as $(1 - \alpha) x_j + \alpha \Phi_j$ with $\alpha = 1 - (1 - \epsilon)^2$, where $x_j$ is the exact $K_{m,n}$ value. Densification added $a = \min(\lfloor \epsilon M \rfloor, C)$ edges among the $C = \binom{m}{2} + \binom{n}{2}$ within-fold vertex pairs, so that each pair was added with probability $q = a / C$. It evaluated $\Phi$ with degrees $(n + q(m - 1), m + q(n - 1))$, class sizes $(m, n)$, and $M' = M + a$. It then multiplied the $K_{m,n}$ articulation points, bridges, and assortativity by $1 - q$ and set clustering to $\min\{c_0 + (1 - c_0) q, 1\}$, where $c_0$ is the $K_{m,n}$ clustering. All other substrates were perturbed as explicit graphs.
